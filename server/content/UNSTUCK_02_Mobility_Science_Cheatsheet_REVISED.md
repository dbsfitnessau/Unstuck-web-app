## **A two-line safety reminder**

Mobility work is uncomfortable at end range. It is not painful in the medical sense. **Sharp, radiating, or progressive pain is a stop sign — see a physio, not your foam roller.** This Cheatsheet is general education; the full disclaimer and contraindications live in the Main Program. Read those before Day 1.

Pregnant or early postpartum: this version of the program isn't for you. Wait for the companion plan or work with a women's health physio.

## The 5 principles of mobility

### 1. A lot of what you feel as "tightness" is a nervous-system signal — not just a short muscle.

When a muscle feels "tight," a significant part of what you're feeling is a protective output from your central nervous system. Your CNS has decided that range is unsafe — usually because you don't visit it often, or you've been hurt there before — and it stiffens you up to keep you out of it. Tissue mechanics matter too, but stretch *tolerance* (how much range you'll allow before your nervous system fights back) changes faster than tissue *length* does.[^1]

This is why long, slow, breathed work in a position changes more than aggressive cranking. You're partly retraining the nervous system to allow the position — and partly working on viscoelastic and connective-tissue properties underneath.

**Practical:** Long exhales. Time under tension. Don't force depth — *earn* it.

\[ILLUSTRATION PLACEHOLDER — Simple icon: brain → spinal cord → muscle, with arrows showing inhibitory signal.\]

### 2. Joints adapt to the angles you load them at.

Your hip capsule, like every connective-tissue structure, remodels in response to the loads and angles you put it through — the soft-tissue version of Wolff's law.[^2] If your daily load history is "sitting, walking, parallel squat," your hip thinks that's the entire job description.

End-range work — the deep squat hold, the Cossack, the loaded couch stretch — sends a signal to the capsule and tendons: *we visit here now, build for it*. That signal takes weeks, not sessions. The 28 days of UNSTUCK banks the early gains; the structural remodelling continues well past Day 28, which is why the maintenance dose matters.

**Practical:** Range you don't load is range you lose. Visit your end ranges every week, deliberately.

\[ILLUSTRATION PLACEHOLDER — Hip joint in three positions: short, mid, end-range, with capsule fibres adapting visually.\]

### 3. Passive range and active range are different — and the gap is where you get hurt.

**Passive range:** what someone (or gravity) can push you into. **Active range:** what you can produce yourself, under your own control.

Strength deficits in long, lengthened positions are a well-documented risk factor for non-contact soft-tissue injury — hamstring strains are the most-studied example.[^3] A hamstring that lengthens passively to the floor but can't produce force at end range is exposed when you sprint or change direction.

CARs (introduced in Week 1) and PAILs/RAILs (added in Weeks 3–4) close this gap directly. They train you to *own* end range, not just visit it.

**Practical:** Passive flexibility without active strength is decorative. Train both.

\[ILLUSTRATION PLACEHOLDER — Two figures side by side: one being pushed into a stretch (passive), one producing the position alone (active), with the gap between labelled "where injuries live."\]

### 4. Tendons and capsules adapt slower than muscles.

Muscle protein synthesis responds to a session within hours. Tendons, ligaments, and joint capsules adapt on roughly an 8–12 week timescale at high loading magnitudes.[^4] This is why a single yoga session leaves you feeling looser but six months of weekly low-load yoga doesn't always change your structure.

The 28-day programme is built around this reality, not around it. The first two weeks lean on nervous-system gains (faster, less durable). Weeks 3–4 start the connective-tissue work (slower, more durable). Both adaptations run in parallel from Day 1 — the emphasis shifts, the biology doesn't switch. The structural remodelling continues for weeks beyond Day 28, which is exactly why the maintenance plan in the Main Programme matters.

**Practical:** Don't expect huge structural change before Week 3. The slow-cooker phase is where the gains stick — and the cooker keeps running long after Day 28 if you keep the maintenance dose going.

\[ILLUSTRATION PLACEHOLDER — Bar chart of adaptation timelines: muscle (hours–days), nervous system (days–weeks), tendon/capsule (weeks–months).\]

### 5. Mobility responds to recovery, not just to work.

Connective tissue adaptation is an active biological process — protein synthesis, collagen turnover — and it needs the same recovery inputs as every other adaptation. Sleep restriction blunts recovery and protein synthesis broadly,[^5] and poor recovery shows up in mobility gains the same way it shows up in strength gains.

Run a 28-day mobility programme on top of poor sleep and red-lined training and you'll get a fraction of the gains.

If a re-test goes backwards, the answer is almost never "stretch harder." It's almost always "sleep more, eat enough, train less around it for a week."

**Practical:** Treat mobility like every other adaptation — work, recover, repeat.

## The vocabulary you'll see in the programme

| Term | What it means | Why you care |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| **CARs** (Controlled Articular Rotations) | Active, full-range circles of one joint, slow and deliberate. Daily input from Week 1. | Trains the joint to own its full range under your own control |
| **PAILs** (Progressive Angular Isometric Loading) | At end range, isometrically push the *stretched tissue* into the floor or wall for ~20s at moderate effort | Builds force production at end range |
| **RAILs** (Regressive Angular Isometric Loading) | At end range, actively pull yourself deeper using the *opposite* muscles for ~20s | Trains active control of new range |
| **End range** | The deepest position you can get into | Where adaptation lives. Where injuries also live if untrained. |
| **Closed-chain** | Hand or foot fixed (e.g. squat, push-up) | More stable, more carryover to athletic positions |
| **Open-chain** | Limb moves freely (e.g. seated leg raise) | Useful for isolation, less carryover |
| **Joint capsule** | The connective sleeve around a joint | The deepest layer of "tightness." Slowest to adapt. |
| **Fascia** | Connective tissue that wraps everything | Real, important, but probably not what Instagram tells you it is |
| **Active control** | Force production *you* generate at a given range | The whole point of this programme |

CARs, PAILs and RAILs come from Functional Range Conditioning (Dr. Andreo Spina). The underlying ideas — end-range isometrics and active control training — have support in the broader strength and rehab literature; the specific FRC protocols are a useful, structured way to apply them.

## What this programme is NOT doing

To save you Googling later:

- It's **not "releasing fascia"** in the way Instagram says. Foam rolling does help — but its acute effects are neurophysiological (modulating mechanoreceptors and pain), not mechanical deformation of fascia. The forces required to actually deform dense fascia are far beyond what a foam roller or hand can produce.[^6]
- It's **not lengthening muscle fibres** in any dramatic way. Short- and medium-term flexibility gains are dominated by changes in stretch tolerance and tissue compliance, not sarcomere count.[^1] Small structural changes (fascicle length, tendon stiffness) can accumulate over months of high-volume work, but that's not what's driving your Week 2 win.
- It's **not going to fix structural issues**. If you have a labral tear, FAI, a herniated disc, or a torn rotator cuff, you need a physio, not a programme.

What it *is* doing: training your nervous system to trust new positions, loading your capsules and tendons through angles they don't normally see, and building active control across a wider range. That is the actual mechanism. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling you a $40 ball.

## The five-minute version

If you remember nothing else from this page:

1. **Slow and breathed beats hard and forced.** Long exhales win.
2. **Visit end range every week, on purpose.** No end range, no adaptation.
3. **Passive flexibility is decorative; active flexibility protects you.** Train both.
4. **Tendons take weeks. Be patient through Week 2.**
5. **Sleep is mobility training.** Don't out-train your recovery.

## **FAQ — The questions everyone asks**

**What if I miss a session?** One missed session in 28 days is noise — pick up where you left off, don't stack two sessions to compensate. Two or more missed sessions in a week, restart the week. The point is consistent stimulus, not heroics.

**Should I train through DOMS?** Mobility, yes — it's often the best thing for sore muscles. Skip the loaded end-range work if soreness is sharp or limits clean form. Drop a tier and keep moving.

**What if I'm sick?** Above the neck (head cold, mild sore throat) — keep moving on 🟢, skip the breath holds. Below the neck (chest, fever, body aches) — rest fully until symptoms resolve, then resume from where you stopped.

**Can I split a session into two short blocks?** Yes. 10 minutes morning + 10 minutes evening works — keep the order (CARs first, breath last).

**Can I do this alongside lifting / running / Hyrox prep?** That's exactly the point. See the "When to slot mobility around your other training" guidance in Part 3. Don't stack a long deep-squat hold immediately before max-effort squatting.

**What if I'm travelling and have no equipment?** Run the 🟢 tier of every session. The bodyweight version of this program is a complete program.

**What if I'm pregnant or postpartum?** See the disclaimer. Don't run this version of the program — wait for the companion plan or work with a women's health physio on a custom adaptation.

**Can I keep the maintenance dose forever?** Yes. That's the design. Re-run the full 28 days when a quarterly retest slips, after an injury or extended layoff, or before a big training block.
